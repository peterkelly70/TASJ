General Comments
This is to document the way TravellerWorlds (old version: the T5 World Builder) builds world details.  I also document influences and discuss detailed world building for T5.


At present, TravellerWorlds uses the MegaTraveller World Builder’s Handbook, Grand Survey, Grand Census, and Book 6 - Scouts in addition to the T5 Core Rules.  I have made various calls in converting these over to T5.  The present ‘world building’ basically crunches numbers into a bit of a sludge to make something that is useful to a Referee.  


But the idea will be to present a new set of T5 rules for detailing worlds in a star system that includes using the new Extensions in combination with random tables to stimulate the world builder’s imagination.

Preface
World Building is fundamentally an act of imagination.


Traveller has a clear procedure for generating Universal World Profiles and many essays, articles and books have been written about turning those numbers into an interesting setting. Many more have been written detailing actual worlds.


The complex mathematics can make world building dull or inaccessible.


I ask myself why I want to adapt past world building publications such as Grand Survey and Grand Census to Traveller5 especially when many Traveller products evoke great settings with little more than a couple of written paragraphs.


At times I find myself researching physics questions about gas compositions and heat retention and chemical reactions and wonder if I am still having fun researching and coding the T5 World Builder.  The answer is always ‘yes’.


So I offer these procedures with some hesitation: great world building is about producing a setting that is fun for all players at the table.  Why should it get bogged down in details about seasonal variations at all latitudes, chemistry and Newtonian mechanics?


Honestly, I don't know.


I just enjoy encoding the procedures I find in Traveller, assisted by the odd bits of chemistry and physics.  It has the potential to produce deep verisimilitude - and computers to crunch the numbers make the job easier.


Which brings me back to the start: for all the numbers, procedures, attempts at physical accuracy and so on, a great idea for a setting of an adventure trumps a set of numbers every time.

The following are subject to expansion:
1. Carbon Dioxide

11. Fluorine, Nitrogen
The Gas Mix is determined based firstly on atmosphere type (Exotic, Corrosive or Insidious).  To ensure the gas mix library is easily expandable, the gas mix list is analysed to determine which mixes fit the atmosphere type - e.g. any gas mix that includes Fluorine cannot be considered merely exotic, and so will not be selected for Exotic atmospheres.  Some gasses are considered corrosive in certain concentrations or insidious in higher concentrations. These are expressed in parts per million in the gas mix table.  This is used to determine a possible percentage for that atmosphere type.  The script then ‘fills’ the rest of the atmosphere with any remaining gas marked as ‘inert’ in the mix (e.g. Nitrogen).  In addition, if an Exotic atmosphere is irritant because of the roll on the Exotic Atmosphere table, then corrosive atmosphere mixes are allowed, but the amount of corrosive gas is limited to being less than the concentration considered corrosive.


This is a very rough draft based on my extensive research (*cough*wikipedia*cough*).  No interaction is done in this gas mix with pressure - so, for example, a certain about of chlorine would be considered corrosive at certain pressures but the same concentration at higher pressures would be insidious.  This is difficult to program and an iterative release and then refining is the best way of doing this.


Anyone with more knowledge of the chemistry is welcome to critique this list, and anyone is welcome to add to the gas and gas mixes list.
Development Note on Atmospheric Composition
At present the composition for Exotic, Corrosive and Insidious atmosphere is too complex for a world building publication.  I would propose to simplify it.


Have the temperature and pressure of the atmosphere calculated separately; assume that the greenhouse effect as calculated includes a denser atmosphere or having more greenhouse gasses present.


Then present a table of reasons including particular gas mixes that the world builder is free to choose or randomly select.  This could be as extensive as a d66 list and include particular damage effects over particular periods of time (e.g. Suffocate-1 every Combat Round or Radiation-3 every hour etc.).  The table can be engineered to classify particular scenarios as fitting particular temperatures and pressures.  It needs to be practical for a Referee who just wants to choose a good-sounding reason on-the-fly (MOARN!).

Hydrosphere Related
Hydrographic Percentage
Precise hydrographic percentage is the Hydrographic UWP digit times 10 plus a flux roll.
Liquid Composition
Liquid composition is based on the atmosphere; generally it is water but in exotic, corrosive or insidious atmospheres a randomly chosen liquid based on liquids associated with various atmospheric gasses is chosen.  See the Gas Mix table for the liquids associated with each gas.
Stress Factor
The Stress Factor provides a general chance of seismic activity in a 24-hour period.  In Grand Survey this is a 4D roll for 32+, adding the stress factor.  This is equivalent to 4D being less than the stress factor minus 4.
Planets
Start with a roll of 1D-3.
If the world is Molten Core, add a roll of 1D-3.
If the world is Heavy Core, add a roll of 1D-2.
For each satellite, add the satellite diameter in km divided by the orbit distance in world radii and divided again by 64.
Add the stellar mass (in Sol masses) divided by the orbital distance in AU.
Satellites
As for planets, except do not add the ‘satellite’ modifier.  Instead, add the planet diameter in km divided by the orbit distance in planet radii and divided again by 64.
NB: this is an experimental measure not in Grand Survey but designed to give satellites a stress factor based on the planet they were orbiting.  This generally results in close satellites of gas giants having extreme stress factors.


Native Life
The basic roll is 2D for 10+ for there to be native life.  If the roll is failed, this does not mean there is no life at all; the life could all be exotic to that world.


DMs: Atmosphere 0: -3; Atmosphere 4-9: +4; Hydrographics 0: -2; Hydrographics 2-8: +1; Average temperature less than -20ºC or more than 30ºC: -1; Central star spectral class G or K: +1; Spectral class F, A, or B: -1.


NB: This has been taken from Grand Survey directly; however, it is possibly contradicted by the Native Intelligent Life from T5; the NIL could indicate native life while this indicator comes out false.  The T5 indicator over-rides the Grand Survey rule; if there is Native Intelligent Life, there is native life.  This could be modified by changing the T5 rules slightly: there could be two new NIL categories: “Established transplants” and “Exotic established transplants” - these cases would arise only if all the conditions for Natives or Exotic Natives were met but the Native Life indicator was ‘No’.  Within the OTU there are thousands of worlds settled by Major Races where there are established populations in the hundreds of millions or higher - Humanitii, Vargr, Aslan, K’Kree, Hiver to name a few of the larger ones.

Population Related
Distribution of Population - not to be implemented
Grand Survey provides a procedure for determining the number and population level of cities.  T5 simply indicates one city per triangle if population is more than 7, and one location of a town if population is less than 7.  Neither of these options close off further cities, towns, villages or hamlets spread across the globe; but the exact nature is better dealt with by the Referee’s imagination - I do not think rolling for the number of major cities adds anything to world building.


This procedure is not implemented in the T5 World Builder.  I am open to persuasion but I don’t think it adds anything to the process we don’t already have.
Population in Orbit
Not implemented yet.  This is more interesting and possibly worth noting for world flavour.
Scenario 1: world barely habitable
If Atmosphere is 10, 11 or 12 and there is no native life, and TL 9+, roll 3D < Population+3.  If successful, TL-6 of the population (that is, people) live permanently in orbit.
Scenario 2: Class A or B Starports
For Starport class A, there may be at least one orbital facility or city with (Population - 2) inhabitants permanently in orbit.  Roll 3D < Population for this to be the case.  This is in addition to a possible highport.


For class B, it is at least one orbital facility or city with (Population - 3) inhabitants in addition to any highport.  Roll 3D < Population for this to be the case.
Scenario 3: Class C Starports
Class C Starports mean there are generally not substantial orbital facilities (except for Referee fiat) but a highport might still be present based on the T5 Core rules.

Strangeness
This is Thomas Jones-Low on CoTI although I can’t find the link - it may have been part of the discussion already mentioned:


This is a composite trait descriptor generated by the IISS for several groups within the Imperium for interfacing purposes. It is a combination of Disposition, Innovation, and Tractability. Strangeness is generated by a flux roll + 5, giving a range of 0 to 10 (A). Low strangeness indicates the culture is close to Imperial norms or understanding of them. High strangeness indicates the culture has very divergent norms, or lacks the disposition to be tolerant of the Imperial culture.


It was originally devised by the Travellers Aid Society (TAS) as an aid to their members to have an understanding of the ease or difficulty their members would have in contacting the world. The original version was a simple score assigned by members during their interactions on the world. The Imperial Navy also devised a similar rating system for their strategic planning. The IISS diplomatic branch currently derives the rating for worlds and gives the information to TAS, Navy, local nobility, and other interested parties.


Symbols
Thomas Jones-Low, brilliantly again - original post is at http://www.travellerrpg.com/CotI/Discuss/showpost.php?p=577390&postcount=29 
This would give a numerical indication of the Progressiveness Attitude and Action in one statistic but with a specific meaning able to be inferred from the technological level.  When calculating the number of novel customs, Symbols is used instead of TL because Symbols represents that society’s cultural TL.


Symbols could also be used to interact with TL as to how the local culture uses technology.  The Symbols rating becomes the effective locally used technology, but it is modified for Tech Level Stages.


For example, a TL 10 society has Symbols of 12.  Travellers to the world will see that the local technology includes many examples of TL12 Prototypes (Technology chapter, p. 495 T5.09 Core Rules).  This society has a lot of ‘early adopters’ of prototypes.  This reflects the local progressive attitude to technology.  On the other hand, a TL7 society might have Symbols of 3; what is in daily use tends to be TL3 Ultimate examples (“if it ain’t broke, don’t fix it” being the prevailing attitude, although older things are improved by design).


When I was trying to understand the HASS, the Symbols threw me for a while too. And then I started to look at this this way.


In order to maintain, or increase, Technology level (the basis of Symbols rating), you need to have the populace understand the technology they are working with every day.


For example, A TL 7 technology uses ground cars. There is a whole set of symbols around operation of a ground car: road signs, markings on the road for which lane to use, instruments on the dashboard, lights on the other cars, and so on.


If you go read stories of the early 19th century about when cars first started to show up, people had no idea what to do with them. They lack the symbology, or knowledge of, cars and all the parts how to interact with them.


So to have a stable TL-7 society everyone needs to know about these things as part of their education, all of the part of the society they are operating in. Cars, communications, schools, government, commerce, social situations. These all have rules, signs, and expectations of how to interact with them.


So the flux roll of 0 on the Symbols says most everyone gets the world as constructed. Everyone knows what a car is, how it operates, rules of the road, how to see what the other cars are doing, etc. Even if they don't operate the vehicles themselves.


When the flux roll deviates from the median (0 point) it would mean the larger and larger parts of the population don't have an understanding of the standard conventions of technology level.


For example, a Symbols of 2 on a TL7 world would imply that almost everyone has never seen a ground car, let alone know how to operate one. There would be few, if any roads, and those that do exist lack basic road markings, and diving on one would be experience as the few ground cars you would meet have different expectations of how the rules of the road operate.


On a Symbols 12 / TL 7 world, every road is clearly marked with signs, lights, and lines. Every citizen is given refresher courses on driving.


So symbols describes how well people interact with the expected technology of their own world. Strageness says how different are those rules from the expected Imperial (or external) standards.


Car example: In Britain, you drive on the left side of the road, whereas in America you drive on the right. But most of the other rules are the same. So a little strange, but both have high symbols ratings.


Because learning to drive a car requires learning the whole set of symbology around cars, it becomes easy to reverse this idea. Worlds where the Symbols are behind the technology level are failing to teach their citizens (for whatever cultural reasons) all of the things they need to operate in their own world. They are falling back technologically.


Conversely, worlds where symbols are ahead of the technology level are more aggressively teaching their citizens about the parts of their world. They will be advancing technologically.


Most of the Imperial citizens have a symbology rating of somewhere between 10 and 14. Or probably based upon the TL of their home world. Members of the Imperial armed forces (Navy and Scouts especially, but also Marines and Army) will have a symbology rating of 15 (imperial maximum).


If your character has (for example) an understanding of TL 12, going to the Symbols 2 / TL 7 world will seem like utter chaos. Sure the technology is understandable (mostly), but no one seems to have the same knowledge.


Conversely on a Symbols 12 / TL 7 world, they will fit right in. Sure the technology is a little backwards but everything is clear, easy to understand, works as expected.


And on a Symbols 20 / TL 15 world, it would be mindboggling. Everything is marked in weird ways that make no sense. The order of operations to get anything done is obscure and rules are confused. Clearly there is order to everything, but you have no idea how to manage anything.

Law Level Profile
Grand Census suggests rolling 2D-7+Government for each area of the law to create a legal profile for the world:
* Weapons
* Trade
* Criminal
* Civil
* Freedom


I will think about the value of this: I am not wholly convinced.  There are many articles expanding usefully on Law Level that are better prods to the imagination than an expanded profile.  But it could lead to interesting variations in government and law.  It could also be a headache for no reason in the many worlds with smaller (Population 4 or less) populations.


To mirror Grand Census, the roll would be Flux + Government for each part of the law level profile.  But I propose the five rolls be Flux + Law Level so that the UWP law level figure remains an average or median of these five law levels.


Culture - Customs
We can quickly roll several customs from Grand Census for local flavour.  The Grand Census procedure is to roll 1D - an even result means generate one custom, and an odd result generate 2.  If TL < 7 then generate one more (i.e. 2 or 3).


I propose we generate one custom if Strangeness is 0-5 or two customs if 6-10, and generate one more if Symbols is less than 7 (which is adopted as the intersection between technology and a society’s adoption of technology into its semiotics).
